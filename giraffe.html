This is the story of a company, Familiar Impossible Dream Logic (FIDL)(TM), that uses AI to generate dream logic. The AI behind FIDL, like all extant generative models, had to first be trained on a dataset but, dreams retold are always something of a disaster in their retelling - always losing their particular, peculiar characteristics. So, taking a cue from DNA collection companies, FIDL decides to offer participants the opportunity to curate, collect, archive their very own dreams (with the fine print reading something to the effect of: by participating, users consent to all resulting data being used by FIDL).

In reality, the dataset actually used for this project is a collection of my own nightmares. I have always had something of a nightmare problem. One of The Internet’s recommendations for this is to write your nightmares down, which is tricky since, as FIDL recognizes, narrative and baseline coherence isn’t native to dream logic. Nonetheless, this technique was helpful and I now have an admittedly bizarre natural language dataset that I call my “Nightmare Catalog”.

The main video component of this project (below) comes from a cocktail of generative methods to make new nightmares from the current dataset - because making new nightmares seemed a more engaging way to explore the latent space of my extant nightmares.

The uncanny nature of many AI generated output is often commented on, and often seen as a shortcoming; a failure to attain accuracy or to depict reality. Models also “hallucinate”. This is mostly understood as data error, data bias, or a failure to balance metrics for novelty against metrics for usefulness (though the specifics are still opaque). It often seems to me that humans suffer from similar afflictions.

In art, we can make use of them, and in our dreams we subconsciously push things into the surreal. Part of my interest in things like FIDL comes from how humans understand machines through these human cognition metaphors, like ‘learning’ and ‘hallucination’ and ‘creativity’. I’m not interested in arguing against this habit - it’s more that I like to keep the human-machine border blurrier from both directions.
